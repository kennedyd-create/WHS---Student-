Trad Study

No AI here — just study techniques backed by cognitive science research (Dunlosky et al., 2013 and others). Rereading and highlighting feel productive, but research consistently shows they barely help you remember anything. These do.

🔥 Do These First (High-Utility)

➕ Also Worth Knowing

🎯 Practice Testing

High utility

Rereading your notes feels like it's working because the material starts to look familiar — but that familiarity is a trap. Recognising a page isn't the same as being able to produce the answer from memory, and exams need the second one. Actively trying to recall something, even when you get it wrong, does far more to lock it into long-term memory than reading it again ever will.

How to do it

  1. After a lesson, pull out the key facts, definitions, and processes and turn each into a question on a flashcard or in a notebook.
  2. Keep each question to one idea only — split up anything with several parts.
  3. Answer fully from memory before you check — give it a real 10-15 seconds of effort, don't peek early.
  4. Check your answer against your notes and fix anything you got wrong or missed.
  5. Sort your questions by how well you knew them, so you know what needs more work.

Example — Biology

After a lesson on mitosis, write "What are the four phases of mitosis, and what happens in each one?" on a card. Say the full answer out loud before flipping it over — not just "prophase, metaphase..." but what's actually happening at each stage.

Example — History

After a lesson on the Treaty of Waitangi, write "What were the key differences between the English and Te Reo Māori versions of the Treaty, and why did they matter?" Answer it fully from memory before checking your notes.

📦 Spaced Repetition — The Leitner Box

High utility

Cramming everything the night before means your brain treats none of it as important. Spacing your reviews out — with a bit of forgetting in between — forces your brain to work harder to retrieve it, which is exactly what makes the memory stick. The Leitner Box is a simple physical (or digital) system for deciding what to review and when, based on how well you actually know it.

The 5 boxes

  • Box 1 — review every day
  • Box 2 — every 2-3 days
  • Box 3 — once a week
  • Box 4 — every 2 weeks
  • Box 5 — once a month, or right before the exam

Get a card right and it moves up a box (a longer wait until you see it again). Get it wrong at any box — even Box 5 — and it goes all the way back to Box 1. That's the whole point: forgotten information gets hammered with review until it's actually secure again.

Example — Chemistry

You get a formula wrong, so it sits in Box 1 and you see it every day. Nail it three days running and it graduates to Box 2, then Box 3 the next time you get it right — but one slip at any point sends it straight back to Box 1, no matter how far it had climbed.

Example — French

New vocab words start in Box 1 and get reviewed daily. Words you consistently get right climb through the boxes and only need a monthly check-in — but any word you slip up on drops straight back to daily review until it's solid again.

🗣️ Teach It (The Protégé Effect)

High utility

When you know you're about to explain something to someone else, your brain automatically starts organising the material differently — picking out what actually matters, building it into a logical order, and connecting it to things you already know. You do this preparation even before you open your mouth. Explaining out loud to a real person works best, because their confused face or follow-up question exposes gaps that silent studying never would.

How to do it

  1. Go in with the mindset: "I need to explain this to someone with zero background knowledge."
  2. Write a one-page outline in plain language — swap jargon for everyday analogies.
  3. Explain it out loud, face to face, without looking at your notes.
  4. Ask your listener to interrupt whenever something's unclear, and to repeat the idea back in their own words.
  5. Afterwards, note exactly where you hesitated or used jargon instead of a real explanation — go fix those specific gaps straight away.

Example — Science

Explain photosynthesis to a younger sibling without using the word "chlorophyll" until you've already explained what that green stuff actually does.

Example — Economics

Explain supply and demand to a parent using something they actually buy — like why petrol prices jump right before a long weekend — instead of just defining the terms.

No one around to listen? The "Rubber Duck" version still works — explain it out loud to literally anything on your desk. Saying it out loud forces your brain to turn vague half-thoughts into real, structured sentences, which is most of the benefit even without an audience.

🤔 Ask "Why?" (Elaborative Interrogation & Self-Explanation)

Moderate utility

Instead of just accepting a fact as true, stop and ask yourself why it's true — this forces your brain to connect it to things you already understand, rather than storing it as an isolated, easily-forgotten detail. Self-explanation is the same idea applied to problem-solving: narrate your reasoning out loud as you work, rather than solving in silence.

Example — Biology

Instead of just memorising "veins have one-way valves, arteries don't," ask: "why would a low-pressure vessel need valves when a high-pressure one doesn't?" Working that out yourself makes the fact far stickier than just reading it.

Example — Geography

Instead of just noting "cities near rivers grow faster," ask: "why would being near a river actually help a city grow — what does it provide that a city further away doesn't get?"

Try this at the end of every section: ask yourself "why does this happen?" and "how does this connect to what I learned last week?" — and during maths or science problems, say your reasoning for each step out loud before you write it down.

🔀 Mix It Up (Interleaved Practice)

Moderate utility

Doing 10 of the same question type in a row (blocked practice) feels easy and builds false confidence, because you already know which method to use before you've even read the question. Mixing different topics or problem types together (interleaved practice) feels harder and slower at first — but it's the only way to actually practise choosing the right method, which is the skill exams really test.

Example — Maths

Instead of doing 10 quadratic equations, then 10 trig problems, then 10 simultaneous equations, build one set that mixes all three types together in a random order — the way they'll actually appear in an exam.

Example — English

Instead of practising essay planning for one text at a time, mix planning questions from all three of your English texts into a single practice session so you're used to switching between them quickly.

💭 The Blurting Method

Diagnostic tool

A quick way to see exactly what you do and don't know about a whole topic, without needing an app or pre-made questions.

  1. Spend 10-15 minutes properly reading one topic section.
  2. Close every notebook, textbook, and slide.
  3. Set a timer for 5-10 minutes and "blurt" — write or draw everything you remember about it, non-stop, no editing.
  4. Reopen your notes and, using a different coloured pen, add anything you missed and fix anything wrong.

The different-coloured additions are a direct map of your gaps — turn those straight into flashcards for Box 1 of your Leitner system.

Example — Physics

After reading about forces and motion, close the book and blurt out every formula, diagram, and worked example you can remember for 5 minutes — then check what you missed on Newton's Third Law specifically.

Example — Social Studies

After a unit on a social issue, blurt out every cause, effect, and perspective you can recall — the gaps that show up usually reveal which viewpoint you understood the least.

🚫 Fix Your Bad Habits

Low utility → fixable

These habits aren't useless because you're doing them wrong — they're just naturally passive. Small changes turn them into something that actually builds memory.

✏️ Highlighting

Instead of: colouring half the page. Try: highlight one phrase per page max, then immediately turn it into a flashcard where you explain it in your own words.

📖 Rereading

Instead of: reading it a third time. Try: read it once, close the book, and say the main argument out loud from memory before you're allowed to reopen it.

📝 Copying Out Summaries

Instead of: copying sentences almost word for word. Try: write your summary completely closed-book first, then open the text only to check accuracy and fill gaps.

📅 A 7-Day Study Routine

No single technique above is enough on its own — real results come from combining them across a week rather than picking one and cramming it.

Days 1-2

Acquisition

Read the material, ask "Why?" questions as you go, and turn key facts into flashcards for Box 1.

Days 3-4

Spaced Retrieval

Review your Leitner boxes, and run a Blurting session on anything dense.

Day 5

Teach It

Explain the week's core ideas to a parent or friend, face-to-face, without notes.

Days 6-7

Mix It Up

Do interleaved practice sets mixing this week's topics with older ones, and advance any Leitner cards you've mastered.
